There is a simple physical truth behind the ability to hold a handstand: the constant, *inevitable* pull of gravity. 

For a long time, my engineering background led me to think of handbalancing as a problem of mechanical equilibrium. And for years, practicing a handstand felt remarkably similar to solving a physics problem. I approached every movement as if it were a system of equations to solve. 

Which muscles should I recruit for a given task? *Which variables compose this system?*
Which is the most important? *Which is the key variable?*
How can I engage the strongest and most natural kinetic chain without introducing compensations? *Which method is the most effective?*

Actually, beneath the apparent stillness, handstands are literally not designed for you as a biped — they are not the best for your shoulder joints, and not a position the human body is designed for — and a vibrant orchestra of variables play their role in this symphony. And to understand how, we first need a model that captures the problem.
What surprises me most is the body’s ability to reorganize and adapt, even when faced with such an unusual, functionally useless, mentally draining and frustrating task.

Therefore, over the years, my all-consuming side project has been refining this model to make it fit the handstand dilemma, and ultimately to find the perfect solution to the system. And practicing handstands, of course. 

Note: I am not a professional handbalancer: what follows is simply a summary of the mental rabbit hole I have been exploring so far. It may contain biases and conceptual errors due to my lack of experience.

### Why a model?

Let's start with a short definition. A model is a simplified formalization of a real phenomenon or problem, expressed in a language that can be analyzed and that retains only the relevant variables. The first step is therefore choosing the level of abstraction: we need to determine which variables are relevant. Then we have to represent them in a language that allows precise treatment (e.g. equations).
On the other hand, models are valid only within the domain they were built for, and if reality departs from their assumptions, the model must be revised.

Applied to the reality of handbalancing, we can see that there are many different handstand shapes. Therefore, if someone asked you, “What is the best model for mathematically describing a person holding a handstand?”, the first answer would be: “Which type of handstand?”
Always start there. If you want to solve a problem, you first need to model it. When talking of handstands, complexity is part of the game. and it's everywhere.

Note: from now on, to model which torques act and where, we will consider the three anatomical planes onto which human movement can be projected: the frontal (or coronal) plane, the transverse (or horizontal) plane, and the sagittal (or median) plane (Figure 1).

With that in mind, we have the first ingredient of the model: a way to say where things happen. The next step is to pick a specific handstand shape and try to build the model: which variables compose the system, which are the most relevant, how formalize and solve the problem.
And, as we know, answers will be valid only within the assumptions we chose.
