Models help us understand the mechanics of a handstand, but how should the body adjust its position to maintain balance? A multibody model helps us see the complete mechanical picture. To effectively restore equilibrium, adjustments must be applied in the right direction, with the appropriate magnitude, and at the right time. 
This is where training comes in: developing the strength, mobility, and coordination required to generate these joint torques is something purely theoretical mechanics cannot do for you. Let us set this aside for now and return to our simplified model, in which the body behaves as an inverted pendulum pivoting about the wrists.

Let $\theta$ be the angular deviation from the perfectly upright position, where positive angles represent a tilt to the right. 
In this simplified setup, the rotational equation of motion is:

$$I\ddot{\theta} = mgh\sin\theta + \tau$$

where

$I$ is the moment of inertia about the pivot, $m$ is the total body mass, $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity, $h$ is the distance from the pivot to the center of mass, and $\tau$ is the control torque generated by the muscles.

For small angular deviations ($\theta \ll 1$), we apply the small-angle approximation $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, yielding:

$$I\ddot{\theta} \approx mgh\,\theta + \tau$$

If the body tilts to the right ($\theta > 0$), a negative torque acts in the opposite direction to counteract the fall. However, simply pushing against the direction of the fall is not always sufficient: the effectiveness of a correction depends on its magnitude, as well as the body’s angular velocity.

To quantify how torque transfers energy into rotational motion, we consider mechanical power ($P$). For a torque acting around a single axis:

$$
P = \tau\,\omega
$$

where $\omega = \dot{\theta}$ is the angular velocity.

Multiplying both sides of the full equation of motion by $\omega$ gives:

$$
I\ddot{\theta}\,\omega = mgh\sin\theta\,\omega + \tau\omega
$$

The left-hand side represents the rate of change of rotational kinetic energy $E_k = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2$ (assuming $I$ is constant):

$$
\frac{dE_k}{dt} = I\omega\dot{\omega} = I\ddot{\theta}\,\omega
$$

The gravitational term corresponds to the rate of change of potential energy. Taking the inverted vertical as our reference ($\theta = 0$), the gravitational potential energy is:
$$
E_p = mgh\cos\theta
$$

Differentiating $E_p$ with respect to time yields:

$$
\frac{dE_p}{dt} = -mgh\sin\theta\,\omega
$$

Rearranging the power balance, we obtain:

$$
\frac{dE_k}{dt} = -\frac{dE_p}{dt} + \tau\omega \implies \frac{d}{dt}(E_k + E_p) = \tau\omega
$$

Defining the total mechanical energy as 

$$E = E_k + E_p
$$

we arrive at the energy rate equation:

$$
\frac{dE}{dt} = \tau\omega
$$

This fundamental relation shows that the rate of change of total mechanical energy equals the power input from the control torque. When the body tilts away from vertical, potential energy decreases. Depending on the direction of motion and the applied torque, kinetic energy may increase or decrease. Thus, the goal of the control torque is not merely to stop the fall instantly, but to manage system energy over time. Without control torque ($\tau = 0$), the system conserves total mechanical energy ($\frac{dE}{dt} = 0$).

This result highlights a key physical insight: balancing on one’s hands is fundamentally a matter of energy management through well-timed, calibrated adjustments—rather than wasting effort in abrupt, inefficient overcorrections.

To illustrate this control mechanism, consider a linear Proportional-Derivative (PD) control law:

$$
\tau = -k_p\theta - k_d\dot{\theta}
$$

Here, $-k_p\theta$ generates a restoring torque proportional to the angular displacement, while $-k_d\dot{\theta}$ provides damping proportional to the angular velocity.

Substituting this control law into the linearized equation of motion yields:

$$
I\ddot{\theta} + k_d\dot{\theta} + (k_p - mgh)\theta = 0
$$

To guarantee asymptotic stability around the vertical equilibrium ($\theta = 0$, $\dot{\theta} = 0$), the system parameters must satisfy two strict conditions:

$$
k_d > 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad k_p > mgh
$$

The condition $k_p > mgh$ ensures that active joint stiffness overcomes the destabilizing pull of gravity, while $k_d > 0$ provides the necessary dissipation to damp out oscillations. Crucially, successful balance relies on tuning these gains to avoid both under-correcting (falling over) and over-correcting (inducing severe, undamped oscillations).
